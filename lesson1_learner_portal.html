🔐 Opening Scenario: The Equifax Data Breach (2017)

A REAL BREACH THAT CHANGED EVERYTHING

147 million people's personal data stolen

What Happened?

Equifax had a known vulnerability for 2 months. They knew exactly what was wrong. But they had NO governance framework to fix it.

The Failure Chain

1. No Governance
Nobody had authority to mandate patches. Different teams managed different systems without coordination.
2. No Risk Assessment
Even if the vulnerability was known, there was no formal process to assess impact and priority.
3. No Patch Management Policy
Without a policy defining timelines, teams didn't know WHEN to patch.
4. No Detective Controls
Even as attackers exploited the vulnerability for 2 months, Equifax had no monitoring to detect it.

The Cost

  • 147 million people affected
  • $700 million settlement
  • CEO resigned
  • Years of reputation damage

The Lesson

This breach could have been prevented with proper governance. The vulnerability didn't require new technology. It required a FRAMEWORK—governance—that told the organization what to do and when to do it.

Reflection: If Equifax had governance, would this breach have happened?

Answer: No. Governance creates the framework that prevents these failures.

Governance & Leadership

What is Governance?

Governance is NOT software. It's NOT a tool you buy. Governance is a decision-making framework that determines WHO makes decisions, WHAT to protect, HOW to protect it, and WHEN to take action.

Governance Definition: A framework of policies, procedures, and accountability structures that ensures an organization systematically identifies, assesses, responds to, and monitors security risks.

The Four Core Questions Governance Answers:

  • WHAT: What assets are critical? What risks do we face? What do we NEED to protect?
  • HOW: How should we protect these assets? What controls should we implement?
  • WHO: Who has authority to make decisions? Who is accountable if something goes wrong?
  • WHEN: When must we take action? What are our deadlines? (e.g., patch critical vulnerabilities within 48 hours)

Why Governance Matters - The Castle Analogy

Imagine defending a medieval castle from attack. You have guards, walls, gates, and various defensive structures. Governance is the battle plan.

WITHOUT governance (battle plan): Guards don't know where to stand. 100 soldiers defend the front gate while 5 soldiers defend the back gate (where the real attack comes). Nobody knows what to do if an attack happens. Attackers break in through the undefended back entrance. Disaster.
WITH governance (battle plan): The commander says: "Gate 1 gets 30 soldiers, Gate 2 gets 40, Gate 3 gets 20." Everyone KNOWS their assignment. If an attack happens on Gate 2, soldiers from Gate 1 and 3 have pre-planned reinforcements. Communication is clear. Roles are defined. The castle is defended effectively.

Key Governance Concepts:

  • Policy: Written rules. Example: "All critical patches must be applied within 48 hours"
  • Procedure: Step-by-step instructions. Example: "1) Identify patch, 2) Test in staging, 3) Deploy to production"
  • Accountability: Clear responsibility. Example: "CISO approves critical patches, System Admin deploys"
  • Audit/Monitoring: Verify the system works. Example: "Check weekly that all critical patches are deployed"

Leadership's Role in Governance

Leadership (CISO, CRO, Board executives) is responsible for establishing and maintaining governance:

  • Define the vision: "Our company follows zero-trust architecture" or "We implement defense in depth"
  • Allocate resources: Budget for security tools, hire staff, fund training
  • Make strategic exceptions: Sometimes accept risk for business reasons. Example: "We'll accept the risk of legacy system because replacing it costs $500K"
  • Resolve conflicts: When operations wants to skip a patch for stability but security says it's critical
  • Set accountability: Define roles. Make clear: "If patches aren't deployed, the system admin is accountable"
  • Report to Board: Present security status to executives and shareholders

Service Level Objectives (SLOs) - How You Measure if Governance Works

SLOs are quantitative, measurable targets that tell you if your governance framework is actually effective:

Example SLOs for Patch Management:
• Critical patches: Applied within 48 hours of release
• High patches: Applied within 7 days
• Medium patches: Applied within 30 days
• Low patches: Applied within 90 days
Example SLOs for Incident Response:
• Detect 95% of breaches within 1 hour
• Contain breach within 4 hours
• Restore systems within 24 hours
Example SLOs for Vulnerability Management:
• Scan all systems daily for vulnerabilities
• Assess severity within 24 hours
• Patch critical vulnerabilities within 48 hours
Example SLOs for Access Control:
• Deactivate terminated user accounts within 4 hours
• Audit privileged accounts monthly
• Reset forgotten passwords within 2 hours

Why SLOs Matter:

WITHOUT SLOs: You have no way to measure if governance is working. Management can't report to the Board: "Is security good?" You have no data. It becomes invisible.

WITH SLOs: You can measure and report: "We patched 100% of critical vulnerabilities within 48 hours this month" or "We detected breaches in 55 minutes on average." You can improve what you measure.

Equifax Example - Governance Failure:

Equifax knew about the vulnerability for 2 months but had NO governance to mandate patches. There was:

  • No CISO with authority to order patches
  • No policy saying "critical patches must be applied within 48 hours"
  • No accountability: If a patch didn't get applied, nobody was responsible
  • No SLOs: No way to measure if patches were being applied

Result: Attackers exploited the vulnerability for 2 months while Equifax knew about it but did nothing.

Risk Management

The Risk Management Process (4 Steps)

  1. Identify: What assets could be at risk? What threats exist?
  2. Assess: How likely is the threat? What's the impact if it happens?
  3. Respond: Choose one of four responses (below)
  4. Monitor: Has the risk changed? Is the control effective?

The Four Risk Responses

1. AVOID
Stop the business activity that creates the risk.
Example: "We don't store credit cards. We use a payment processor."
2. ACCEPT
Choose to live with the risk because business benefits outweigh it.
Example: "We'll accept a 4-hour downtime risk on Sunday to patch systems"
3. MITIGATE
Implement controls to reduce likelihood or impact.
Example: "Apply patches within 48 hours" (reduces likelihood)
4. TRANSFER
Shift the risk to a third party.
Example: "Buy cyber insurance" or "Outsource payment processing"

How to Choose a Response

Likelihood Impact Best Response
High High MITIGATE
High Low MITIGATE
Low High MITIGATE or AVOID
Low Low ACCEPT

Security Controls

What is a Security Control?

A security control is any measure taken to protect an asset from a threat. Controls can be technology-based, people-based, or governance-based. The most effective security uses ALL THREE TYPES working together.

The 3x3 Framework: Categories and Functions

Security controls can be organized two ways:

PART 1: THREE CONTROL CATEGORIES

Every control falls into one of three categories:

TECHNICAL CONTROLS
Technology-based safeguards. Implemented in software/hardware.
Examples: Firewalls, encryption, antivirus, MFA (multi-factor authentication), IDS (intrusion detection), VPN, password managers, access controls
OPERATIONAL CONTROLS
People and process-based safeguards. Implemented through training and procedures.
Examples: Security awareness training, incident response procedures, background checks, security drills, access procedures, change management
MANAGERIAL CONTROLS
Governance-based safeguards. Implemented through policies and oversight.
Examples: Security policies, risk assessments, compliance audits, asset inventory, vendor management, SLOs

PART 2: THREE CONTROL FUNCTIONS

Every control also serves one of three functions:

PREVENTIVE CONTROLS
Stop attacks BEFORE they happen. Reduce likelihood of attack succeeding.
Examples: Firewall blocks malicious traffic, MFA prevents unauthorized logins, encryption prevents data theft
DETECTIVE CONTROLS
Detect attacks WHILE they are happening. Allows quick response.
Examples: IDS detects port scanning, SIEM detects unusual login patterns, log monitoring detects file access
CORRECTIVE CONTROLS
Fix damage AFTER attack succeeds. Restore normal operations.
Examples: Restore from backup, incident response team contains breach, patching fixes vulnerability

KEY PRINCIPLE: DEFENSE IN DEPTH (You need all 3 categories!)

Technology alone will fail. People training alone will fail. Governance alone will fail. You NEED all three working together.

REAL WORLD EXAMPLE: Phishing Attack

SCENARIO: Employee receives email saying "Click here to reset your password." It's actually a phishing email from attackers.

TECHNICAL CONTROLS:
✓ Email filter (preventive) - tries to block phishing emails
✓ MFA (preventive) - even if password stolen, attacker needs second factor
✓ SIEM (detective) - notices unusual login from different country

OPERATIONAL CONTROLS:
✓ Training (preventive) - teaches employees: "Don't click suspicious links"
✓ Incident response (corrective) - if someone got phished, team responds

MANAGERIAL CONTROLS:
✓ Policy (preventive) - "Never click links in unsolicited emails"
✓ SLO (detective) - "Detect phishing campaigns within 1 hour"

WHAT HAPPENS:
• Email filter catches 70% of phishing (technical preventive)
• Employee training catches another 20% (operational preventive)
• 10% get through, but MFA stops the login (technical preventive)
• SIEM detects the unusual login attempt (technical detective)
• Incident response team investigates (operational corrective)

WHAT IF WE ONLY HAD TECHNICAL CONTROLS?
Email filter catches 70%, but if employee is tricked by the other 30%, attacker gets in. (No training = more people fall for phishing)

WHAT IF WE ONLY HAD TRAINING?
Employees learn about phishing, but if someone clicks anyway, there's nothing stopping them. (No MFA = no second layer)

WHAT IF WE ONLY HAD POLICY?
Policy says "don't click suspicious links" but nobody monitors if people follow it. (No detective controls = nobody catches the breach)

Why Equifax Failed (Control Perspective):

  • PREVENTIVE: No patching policy (governance) to prevent exploits
  • DETECTIVE: No monitoring to detect attacks
  • CORRECTIVE: No incident response to contain breach

Result: Attackers exploited a preventable vulnerability for 2 months without detection.

Attack Surface Management

What is Attack Surface?

Attack surface = Every possible way an attacker could exploit your systems. It's the sum of all entry points, vulnerabilities, and weaknesses that could allow an attacker to compromise you.

Components of Attack Surface:

  • Open ports: Network services listening for connections (web server on port 80, SSH on port 22, etc.)
  • User accounts: Every account is a potential login point. More accounts = more attack surface
  • Unpatched systems: Known vulnerabilities that attackers can exploit
  • Misconfigured systems: Accidentally exposed data, weak encryption, default passwords
  • Third-party software: Vulnerabilities in libraries, plugins, vendor code
  • Network protocols: Old insecure protocols (Telnet, HTTP, NFS) vs secure ones (SSH, HTTPS, SMB)
  • Human factors: Employees who can be phished, socially engineered

KEY PRINCIPLE: Smaller Attack Surface = Better Security

Think of it like a castle: If you have 10 gates, enemies can attack any of 10 places. If you reduce to 2 gates, enemies only have 2 options. If you reduce to 1 gate with strong defenses, much harder to break in.

Real-World Example: File Server

BEFORE Attack Surface Reduction:
File Server has these services listening:
• Port 21: FTP (old file transfer, unencrypted passwords)
• Port 23: Telnet (remote access, unencrypted)
• Port 111: NFS (network file system, weak security)
• Port 139: NetBIOS (legacy networking)
• Port 445: SMB (Windows file sharing)
• Port 22: SSH (secure shell, OK)

Problem: 6 potential entry points. Attackers can probe FTP, Telnet, NFS looking for exploits.
AFTER Attack Surface Reduction:
File Server has only:
• Port 445: SMB (Windows file sharing, required)
• Port 22: SSH (for admin access, required)

Benefit: Only 2 entry points. Fewer services to exploit. Much more secure. Easier to monitor and audit.

Attack Surface Reduction Strategy - 4 Key Steps:

STEP 1: Disable Unnecessary Services
Actions:
• Audit all running services: "Do we actually use this?"
• If not needed: STOP the service and DISABLE it
Examples to disable:
✗ FTP (use SSH/SFTP instead)
✗ Telnet (use SSH instead)
✗ NFS (use SMB instead)
✗ SNMP v1/v2 (use SNMP v3 with encryption)
✗ HTTP (use HTTPS)
Impact: Every disabled service = one fewer way to attack you
STEP 2: Remove Unnecessary User Accounts
Actions:
• Audit all user accounts: "Who is this person? Do they still work here?"
• Deactivate terminated employees immediately
• Remove test/demo accounts
• Remove shared accounts (each person should have own ID)
Real danger: Former employee's account is compromised or still has access
Impact: Every deleted account = one fewer potential login point
STEP 3: Harden Configurations
Actions:
• Apply CIS Benchmarks (step-by-step hardening guides)
• Apply NIST guidelines
• Apply vendor hardening guides
Examples of hardening:
✓ Disable unused protocols (NFS, NetBIOS)
✓ Require strong passwords
✓ Enable encryption (TLS 1.3)
✓ Disable default accounts
✓ Remove unnecessary software
Impact: Systems are more resistant to known attacks
STEP 4: Patch Continuously
Why: Unpatched systems = known exploitable vulnerabilities
Actions:
• Subscribe to security alerts
• Assess which patches affect you
• Deploy CRITICAL patches within 48 hours
• Deploy HIGH patches within 1 week
Impact: Known vulnerabilities are fixed before attackers exploit them

Why Equifax Failed - Attack Surface Perspective:

Equifax had a vulnerability in their web server. They could have:

  • DISABLED the vulnerable service
  • PATCHED the vulnerability
  • ISOLATED the vulnerable system from the internet

They did NONE of these. The vulnerability remained exposed for 2 months.

Patch Management

What is a Patch?

A patch is a software update that fixes a known security vulnerability or bug. When vendors discover vulnerabilities, they release patches to fix them. Applying patches is one of the most critical security practices.

The 7-Step Patch Management Lifecycle:

Step 1: DISCOVER
A vulnerability is publicly announced by vendor or security researcher. You learn about it via vendor websites, CVE databases, security feeds.
Your action: Subscribe to security alerts for all vendors you use
Step 2: ASSESS
Determine: Do WE have this vulnerability? What systems are affected? Is it critical?
Actions:
• Check which systems use vulnerable software
• Check what version you're running
• Determine CVSS score (how serious)
• Check if actively being exploited
Step 3: PLAN
Schedule when you'll deploy the patch. Get approvals. Prepare rollback plan.
Actions:
• Classify as CRITICAL/HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW
• Schedule deployment window
• Request management approval
• Notify affected users
Step 4: TEST
Test patch in staging environment to verify nothing breaks.
Actions:
• Apply patch to non-production copy
• Test all critical functionality
• Check for unintended side effects
Step 5: DEPLOY
Apply patch to production systems. Usually done in phases to minimize risk.
Actions:
• Back up all systems
• Deploy to small group first
• Monitor for issues
• Deploy to remaining systems in waves
Step 6: VERIFY
Confirm patch installed correctly on all systems.
Actions:
• Run vulnerability scan
• Check version numbers
• Verify functionality
Step 7: MONITOR
Watch for patch-related issues. Monitor for 1+ weeks to ensure stability.
Actions:
• Monitor system performance
• Check application logs
• If issues found, prepare rollback

Patch Classification & Response Timelines:

Classification Definition Timeline Example
CRITICAL Actively exploited OR worm capability 48 hours WannaCry (2017) - patch all within 48h
HIGH Remote code execution possible 1 week Windows RCE - patch within 7 days
MEDIUM Local privilege escalation 30 days Weak encryption - patch within 30d
LOW Denial of Service or minor bug 90 days UI bug - patch within 90d

Patch Management Tools:

  • WSUS: Windows Server Update Services (Microsoft)
  • SCCM: System Center Configuration Manager (enterprise)
  • apt/yum: Linux package managers
  • Ansible/Terraform: Infrastructure-as-Code automation

The Equifax Breach: A Patching Failure Story

Timeline of Failure:
• March 8, 2017: Apache announces critical RCE vulnerability
• March 8 - May 13 (2 MONTHS): Equifax KNOWS about it but doesn't patch
• Attackers actively exploit unpatched Equifax systems
• May 13: Equifax finally discovers the breach
• Result: 147 million people's data stolen, $700M settlement

Why it happened: Equifax got stuck at Step 2 (ASSESS). They discovered the vulnerability but never planned (Step 3) or deployed (Step 5) the patch.

Root cause: NO GOVERNANCE. No policy saying "Critical patches must be applied within 48 hours." No accountability if patches weren't applied. No process to enforce it.

Configuration Management & Infrastructure as Code

The Problem: Configuration Drift

In organizations without configuration management, systems gradually become inconsistent and unpredictable:

Real-world example of drift:
• Web Server 1: SSH enabled, TLS 1.3, updated 2025-01-01
• Web Server 2: SSH enabled, TLS 1.2, updated 2024-12-15
• Web Server 3: SSH disabled, TLS 1.0 (VULNERABLE!), updated 2024-06-01

Result: Different security levels, impossible to audit, one server is vulnerable!

Why Configuration Drift Happens:

  • Manual configuration: Each sysadmin configures servers slightly differently
  • Patches applied at different times: Some get latest patches, some don't
  • Ad-hoc changes: Someone makes an "emergency fix" that isn't documented
  • No audit trail: Hard to know what changed and when
  • Human error: Configuration mistakes creep in

The Solution: Infrastructure as Code (IaC)

Define your entire infrastructure in CODE. When you need new servers, run the code and they're configured identically, securely, and consistently.

Infrastructure as Code Principle: Your infrastructure configuration should be:
✓ Written in code (human-readable)
✓ Version controlled (Git history of changes)
✓ Automated (no manual clicking)
✓ Idempotent (same result every time)
✓ Repeatable (can be deployed anywhere)

Configuration Management Tools:

Chef (Recipe-based)
Uses "recipes" written in Ruby to define configuration.
Example use case: "Recipe to install Apache, enable SSL, configure firewall rules"
Best for: Complex infrastructure with many rules
Puppet (Model-based)
Define desired state in Puppet DSL, Puppet enforces it continuously.
Example use case: "Server should have Apache installed, TLS 1.3 enabled, SSH on port 22"
Best for: Complex, large-scale environments
Ansible (Agentless)
Uses YAML configuration files, pushes changes via SSH.
Example use case: "Install Docker, pull image, run container"
Best for: Simplicity, easy to learn, no agents needed
Terraform (Infrastructure Provisioning)
Provisions actual infrastructure (VMs, networks, storage) on cloud platforms.
Example use case: "Create 10 VMs in AWS, configure security groups, attach storage"
Best for: Cloud infrastructure as code

Hardening Standards - How to Apply IaC Securely:

When you write your IaC code, follow established hardening standards to ensure security:

  • CIS Benchmarks: Center for Internet Security provides step-by-step hardening guides. Example: "CIS Windows Server 2019 Hardening" has 1000+ recommendations
  • NIST Guidelines: National Institute of Standards & Technology provides standards (SP 800-53, SP 800-171)
  • STIGs (Secure Technical Implementation Guides): U.S. Department of Defense standards for hardening systems

IaC Benefits:

  • Consistency: All servers built from same code are identical
  • Security: Hardening is built into code, not afterthought
  • Repeatability: Rebuild servers anytime if needed
  • Auditability: Git history shows all changes
  • Speed: Deploy 100 servers in minutes, not days
  • Documentation: Code IS the documentation

Maintenance Windows

What is a Maintenance Window?

A scheduled, planned period of time when IT systems are taken offline (or partially offline) to apply patches, updates, configuration changes, or system upgrades. Users are notified in advance. Systems are unavailable during the window, which is why windows are scheduled during low-activity times.

Standard Maintenance Window Schedule:

PRIMARY MAINTENANCE WINDOW
When: Every Sunday, 10:00 PM to 6:00 AM Monday
Duration: 8 hours
Frequency: Weekly
Why this time? Minimum business impact - users are sleeping, weekend is slower than weekday
SECONDARY MAINTENANCE WINDOW
When: 2nd Sunday of month, same time (10 PM - 6 AM)
Duration: 8 hours
Frequency: Monthly
Purpose: Catch missed patches from primary, deploy major updates requiring more time

Pre-Maintenance Checklist:

  • Full system backup: Create complete backup BEFORE any changes
  • Document baseline: Record all version numbers, configurations, system state
  • Prepare rollback plan: Detailed steps to revert changes if something breaks
  • User notification (1 week before): Email: "Maintenance window Sunday 10 PM, expect downtime"
  • Final reminder (24 hours before): Remind users, confirm on-call staff available
  • Test in staging: Apply all patches to non-production system first
  • Brief all personnel: Make sure IT staff know their roles

During Maintenance Window - 7-Step Process:

  1. Gracefully stop user access: Disconnect users, display: "System maintenance in progress, check back at 6 AM"
  2. Deploy patches: Apply all tested patches to systems
  3. Test critical functionality: System boots? Key apps respond? Database works?
  4. Security verification: Firewalls in place? Encryption enabled? IDS responding?
  5. Monitor for errors: Watch system logs for ERROR or CRITICAL messages
  6. Bring systems online: Allow users to reconnect
  7. Monitor post-deployment: Watch closely for 1+ hour for any problems

⚠️ MAINTENANCE WINDOWS ARE HIGH-RISK - THE 4 BIG DANGERS:

Maintenance windows are when organizations are MOST VULNERABLE to attack. This is when many breaches happen.
RISK 1: Systems Might FAIL from Patches
A patch might:
• Break an application
• Prevent system from booting
• Corrupt a database
• Cause critical service to crash

Result: System is DOWN and UNDEFENDED during recovery attempts.
RISK 2: Security MONITORING Might be OFFLINE
During maintenance:
• SIEM (Security Information & Event Management) might be patched
• IDS/IPS (Intrusion Detection) might be offline
• Firewalls might be reconfigured
• EDR (Endpoint Detection) agents might be updated

Result: Attackers can exploit systems and go UNDETECTED because monitoring is down.
RISK 3: Authentication/MFA Systems Might be DOWN
Critical systems being patched:
• Active Directory (Windows user authentication)
• LDAP servers
• MFA systems
• Password managers

Result: Emergency access procedures might be needed, which attackers can exploit.
RISK 4: Attackers SPECIFICALLY TARGET Maintenance Windows
• Attackers know companies patch on Sunday nights
• They know monitoring is lighter during windows
• They know staff is tired and rushing
• They launch attacks knowing defenses are weakened

Result: Organized attackers actively scan for maintenance window vulnerability.

Defenses During Maintenance Windows:

  • Out-of-band monitoring: Separate monitoring system (NOT being patched) watches for suspicious activity
  • Multiple approval layers: Require CISO + Operations approval before window starts
  • Dedicated security staff on-call: Have security team available 24/7 during window
  • Detailed logging: Log every change made - create audit trail
  • Quick rollback ability: Be able to revert all changes within 1 hour if critical issue found
  • Staggered deployments: Don't patch everything at once - do in waves to catch problems early
  • Clear communication: Keep all stakeholders informed: "Patch A deployed successfully, B starting now"
  • Randomized window time: Don't always do Sunday - occasionally do different days/times to surprise attackers

Post-Maintenance Window Verification:

  • Verify all systems online: Confirm all applications responding
  • Check security controls: Verify firewalls, detection systems, encryption all working
  • Performance baseline: Compare system performance to pre-window baseline
  • User feedback: Ask: "Is everything working normally for you?"
  • Monitor for 1+ week: Watch for delayed issues or patch side effects
  • Post-incident review (if issues found): "What went wrong? How do we prevent this next time?"

Configuration Management Tools - Detailed Comparison

These tools automate infrastructure management, ensure consistency, and reduce manual configuration errors:

CHEF (Recipe-Based)
How it works: Write "recipes" (Ruby code) that describe how to configure systems
Philosophy: Procedural - describe the steps to reach desired state
Use case example: "Recipe: Install Apache, enable SSL, configure firewall rules, deploy web app"
Best for: Complex infrastructure with many custom rules

Real-world use: Large companies managing thousands of servers
PUPPET (Model-Based)
How it works: Define desired state of system. Puppet continuously enforces it.
Philosophy: Declarative - describe WHAT you want, Puppet figures out HOW
Use case example: "Server should have: Apache installed, TLS 1.3 enabled, SSH on port 22"
Best for: Large complex environments where continuous compliance is critical
Learning curve: Medium - requires Puppet DSL
Real-world use: Enterprise environments, government systems
ANSIBLE (Agentless)
How it works: Write YAML configuration files, push changes via SSH
Philosophy: Simple, agentless - no software needed on target systems
Use case example: "Install Docker, pull image, start container"
Best for: Simplicity, quick learning, multi-platform support
Learning curve: Low - YAML is simple, very straightforward
Real-world use: DevOps teams, cloud environments, rapid deployment
TERRAFORM (Infrastructure Provisioning)
How it works: Define entire infrastructure (VMs, networks, storage) in code
Philosophy: IaC - infrastructure defined as code, version controlled
Use case example: "Create 100 VMs in AWS, configure security groups, attach storage"
Best for: Cloud infrastructure, multi-cloud deployments, entire environment setup
Learning curve: Medium - HCL language is easy
Real-world use: Cloud-native companies, DevOps, infrastructure teams

Hardening Standards & Compliance Frameworks:

CIS Benchmarks™ (Center for Internet Security)
What: Step-by-step hardening guides for systems, applications, cloud platforms
Coverage: Windows, Linux, macOS, Docker, Kubernetes, AWS, Azure, etc.
Example: "CIS Windows Server 2019 Level 1" has 270+ security recommendations
How to use: Follow each recommendation (Enable feature X, Disable feature Y)
Real-world: Standard requirement for government contracts, regulated industries
Website: https://www.cisecurity.org/
STIGs (Security Technical Implementation Guides - DoD)
What: United States Department of Defense hardening standards
Coverage: Windows, Linux, network devices, applications
Example: "Windows 10 STIG" - hundreds of security settings
Who uses: Government agencies, defense contractors, high-security environments
Strictness: More stringent than CIS in some areas
Website: https://public.cyber.mil/
NIST SP 800-53 (National Institute of Standards & Technology)
What: Comprehensive security control framework for federal systems
Focus: Not just "how to configure" but "what controls to implement"
Coverage: 188+ security controls across 14 categories (Access Control, Incident Response, etc.)
Who uses: Federal agencies, contractors, regulated industries
Used with: NIST SP 800-171 (contractors), NIST CSF (all organizations)
Website: https://csrc.nist.gov/publications/detail/sp/800-53

Patch Management Tools:

  • WSUS (Windows Server Update Services): Microsoft's tool for patching Windows systems. Commonly used in organizations to manage Windows updates centrally.
  • SCCM (System Center Configuration Manager): Enterprise-grade tool from Microsoft. Manages updates, software deployment, and system configuration for entire organizations.
  • apt (Debian/Ubuntu Linux): Package manager for Linux systems. Command: `apt update && apt upgrade` applies patches.
  • yum (RedHat/CentOS Linux): Package manager for Linux systems. Command: `yum update` applies patches.
  • Ansible: Can orchestrate patching across heterogeneous environments (Windows + Linux simultaneously).
  • Terraform: Can manage patches as part of infrastructure-as-code (rebuild entire systems with latest patches).

Monitoring & Detection Tools (For Reference):

  • SIEM (Security Information & Event Management): Collects logs from all systems, detects suspicious patterns. Examples: Splunk, ArcSight, IBM QRadar
  • IDS/IPS (Intrusion Detection/Prevention): Monitors network traffic for attacks. Examples: Snort, Suricata, Zeek
  • EDR (Endpoint Detection & Response): Monitors endpoints for threats. Examples: CrowdStrike, Microsoft Defender for Endpoint
